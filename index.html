This Just In: Cable News Sentiment and the Macroeconomy

with Frankie Lin

abstract ↓

To what degree are narratives about the economy self-fulfilling? This question has a long intellectual lineage dating back to Keynes (1936), who emphasized the role of "animal spirits" as a key driver of macroeconomic fluctuations, and has resurfaced prominently in popular discourse through the so-called "vibecession" — a term describing the observed disconnect between economic data and consumer sentiment between 2022 and 2024. The central idea is that negative perceptions of the economy, even when unfounded in current fundamentals, could be self-fulfilling if households behave differently upon exposure to them. In this paper, we investigate how exposure to economic narratives in cable news affects regional economic outcomes. To address the natural endogeneity of economic news to the aggregate economy, we propose a novel identification strategy exploiting geographic variation in media diet across the United States. Cable news diets vary widely across regions in their mix of CNN, MSNBC, and Fox News, and the economic narratives presented by each outlet vary considerably depending on the state of the economy and the political climate. We combine these features to construct a novel measure of regional news exposure that nets out variation in the aggregate economy, with the key identifying assumption that differences in narratives between national cable stations are exogenous to any specific regional economy.

The Effects of Government Spending on Basic and Applied Research

with Robert Remuszka

abstract ↓

It is by now widely recognized that research and development (R&D) spending is a fundamental driver of economic growth. Recent theoretical work has suggested that the kind of R&D spending ought to matter, with a particular focus on the distinction between basic and applied research. What are the returns on these two types of research? How can estimates of these returns help us think about the appropriate allocation of R&D spending between basic and applied research? To answer these questions, we leverage data on the universe of contractual agreements by the United States Federal Government from 2001 to 2023. By linking R&D Product and Service codes (PSC) to NAICS industries, we use county-level exposure to Federal R&D spending to estimate the differential effects of applied and basic research spending.
